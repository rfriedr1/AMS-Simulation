Inside the Cs sputter ion source
Caesium vapour sprayed onto the glowing ionizer leaves as Cs⁺, which the spherical ionizer focuses through the lens aperture onto the sample (10 keV). Every impact blasts atoms out of the graphite — many pick up an extra electron and leave as negative ions: focused by the immersion lens, out through the ionizer hole, trimmed by the extractor, accelerated to 40 keV in the extraction gap, aimed by the steerer and focused by the box lens onto the object point of the LE magnet. Welcome to MICADAS
This is a working model of a real radiocarbon-dating machine. Negative carbon ions race from the ion source (top left) through two magnets, a 200 kV accelerator and an energy filter to the detector. Hover over any component — or detune a slider and watch where the beam ends up.

Inside the source

What you are seeing
Cs vapour — neutral caesium, sprayed from the ring nozzle onto the hot ionizer surface.
Cs⁺ ions — born on the ionizer surface, accelerated and focused onto the sample by the cathode potential.
Negative ions — C⁻ (amber), CH⁻/CH₂⁻ (violet/pink) and O⁻ (cyan) sputtered off the target, accelerated out to the right.
Sputtered neutrals — uncharged debris; no field collects it, it just coats the source.
Electrons — leave the cathode with the anions and are accelerated the same way; the suppressor magnets at the extractor throw them out while they are still slow.
Equipotential lines (dashed) — the electric landscape, from −40 kV at the cathode to 0 V at the exit. One and the same field pulls Cs⁺ towards the sample and the negative ions out of it.

How accelerator mass spectrometry works

The problem. Every living thing constantly takes up carbon — including a radioactive trace of ¹⁴C made by cosmic rays. When an organism dies, the intake stops and the ¹⁴C slowly decays with a half-life of 5730 years. Measure how much is left, and you know how long ago it died. The catch: in a modern sample only one in a trillion carbon atoms is ¹⁴C. Waiting for decays takes grams of material and weeks of counting — AMS instead counts the atoms directly, from less than a milligram, in under an hour.

The impostors. Picking one atom in 10¹² means defeating lookalikes: ¹⁴N (same mass, a million times more abundant) and molecules like ¹³CH and ¹²CH₂ that weigh almost exactly 14 u. AMS beats them in three moves:

  1. Negative ions. The caesium sputter source only emits negative ions — and nitrogen cannot form a stable one. ¹⁴N is out before the race begins.
  2. The stripper. Inside the 200 kV tandem accelerator the ions fly through helium gas that strips electrons off, flipping them to positive (1+). Molecules cannot survive losing these electrons — they are torn apart. Their fragments have the wrong momentum and energy and are swept aside by the HE magnet and the ESA.
  3. Triple filtering. A magnet sorts by mass before the accelerator, a second magnet sorts by momentum after it, and the electrostatic analyzer checks the energy. Only true 440 keV ¹⁴C(1+) survives all three and reaches the detector.

The measurement. The stable beams ¹²C⁺ and ¹³C⁺ are measured as electric currents in Faraday cups, while ¹⁴C is counted atom by atom in a gas ionization detector. The ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio — compared against standards — gives the radiocarbon age.

Watching the impostors: the ¹³CH monitor cup. A tiny fraction of the molecules survives the stripper intact — and a surviving ¹³CH⁺ is indistinguishable from ¹⁴C to every filter in the machine: same mass (to 0.06 %), same momentum, same energy. It cannot be caught directly; it becomes background in the detector. The trick is to count its broken-up siblings instead. Nearly all ¹³CH molecules are torn apart in the stripper, and their ¹³C fragments leave the proton's share of the energy behind — with only 13/14 of the full energy they take a visibly tighter curve through the HE magnet and land on their own unique spot, right between the ¹²C and the ¹³C cup. A third Faraday cup parked there measures this fragment current: a live gauge of how much hydride is riding along with the mass-14 beam, sample by sample. And since the surviving fraction is a fixed property of the stripper, that current — multiplied by a constant calibrated with ¹⁴C-free blank material — predicts the molecular background in the detector. In the measurement panel you can watch the prediction ("predicted mol. bg") track the actual background events. In a real lab this monitor also flags hydrogen-rich, poorly prepared samples the moment they are sputtered.

This machine. MICADAS (MIni CArbon DAting System) was developed at ETH Zurich (Synal, Stocker & Suter, 2007) and is built by Ionplus AG. It is the most compact ¹⁴C-AMS in the world (≈3.2 × 2.6 m): a vacuum-insulated 200 kV tandem, helium stripping, 90° magnets, and a 90° ESA (r = 380 mm, ±40 kV) in front of the detector — exactly the layout and the numbers used in this simulation.

Simulation notes: trajectories use first-order beam optics around the reference path; transverse offsets are exaggerated ~3× for visibility — except neutral atoms, which are drawn on their geometrically true straight line; beam intensities and molecular survival are stylised so that rare events remain visible.
